Causal loop diagrams are visual representations of key factors in a system and the directed relationships among them. I use participatory group model building to co-design these diagrams with stakeholders, translating lived experience, implementation knowledge, and domain expertise into structured causal maps.

This work spans projects on HIV stigma and service engagement in the United States and maternal health service delivery redesign in Kenya. Across both settings, the goal is to understand how feedback loops, barriers, facilitators, and contextual conditions shape health behaviors, service delivery, and intervention outcomes.

## Background

Many health interventions operate within complex systems, where outcomes are shaped by more than the intervention itself. Social norms, trust, stigma, provider behavior, service quality, resource constraints, and policy environments can interact over time in ways that amplify or dampen intervention effects.

Causal loop diagrams provide a way to make these interactions visible. They help organize stakeholder knowledge into feedback structures that show how one part of a system may reinforce or counteract another. In my work, these diagrams are used both as qualitative outputs and as foundations for later quantitative modeling, scenario development, and decision-support tools.

## Methods

The causal mapping process typically begins with a focused research question and an initial set of factors drawn from literature, theory, implementation frameworks, or prior qualitative work. These inputs are used to develop preliminary diagrams or discussion prompts.

Stakeholders then participate in facilitated group model building sessions to revise, expand, and validate the diagrams. During these sessions, participants identify key factors, specify causal relationships, discuss feedback loops, and surface barriers or facilitators that may influence implementation or service engagement.

After the workshops, diagrams are refined through synthesis, expert review, and validation. The final causal loop diagrams are used to identify system domains, leverage points, and hypothesized mechanisms that can inform intervention design, implementation strategy, and quantitative model development.

## Examples

**Mapping HIV stigma across prevention, testing, and treatment**

In one project, I used participatory systems methods to examine how HIV stigma shapes engagement across the HIV care continuum. We conducted virtual group model building sessions with stakeholders from city and state health departments and community-based organizations across Ending the HIV Epidemic priority jurisdictions.

The resulting causal loop diagrams mapped feedback pathways across HIV prevention, testing, and treatment. They highlighted three interconnected domains: personal health attitudes and care navigation, provision of quality services, and community influences on HIV-related health and care.

This work showed how anticipated, internalized, enacted, and community-level stigma can operate through individual, social, and structural pathways. For example, anticipated and internalized stigma may weaken social support and resilience, while enacted stigma from providers can reduce care quality and compromise trust in services. The diagrams help identify potential leverage points for stigma mitigation and multi-level intervention design.

**Mapping implementation pathways for maternal health service delivery redesign**

In another project, I applied participatory causal mapping to Service Delivery Redesign for maternal and neonatal health in Kakamega County, Kenya. This work focused on how a complex health system strengthening intervention could shift deliveries and high-risk pregnancies toward better-equipped facilities while improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Using a theory-of-change approach, we identified factors influencing the demand and supply of maternal and neonatal health services, then mapped their causal relationships through a participatory group model building workshop. The resulting diagrams, **Figure 2** included as an example, helped clarify implementation pathways, barriers, facilitators, and feedback loops across community, facility, referral, and policy levels.

Several key mechanisms emerged. At the community level, trust in the health system reinforced use of quality services. Across facilities, a well-functioning referral system supported timely, coordinated care. At the policy and system level, increasing demand for services needed to be matched by available resources and supportive financing. Across these pathways, trust emerged as a central mechanism connecting co-creation, coordination, service readiness, and sustained improvements in maternal and neonatal outcomes.
